Resilient Cooperative Exploration under Sparse Communication

A decentralized learning architecture and a five-study experimental program on connectivity-aware coverage in obstacle worlds.

Bijan Mehr  ·  working technical note  ·  revised July 2026

Abstract

We study a team of autonomous agents that must map and cover an unknown, obstacle-filled space while remaining connected over a communication graph whose links exist only within a short range. Each agent sees a small patch of the world, remembers what it and its neighbours have seen, and chooses where to go next; no agent has a global view, and no central controller issues commands. The team succeeds or fails at the level of the mission—how much of the space is covered, and whether the group stays in one connected piece—yet every decision is made locally. This note sets out the agent we built to act in that setting and the five experiments we are running to test which parts of it actually do the work.

The architecture combines a size-invariant graph network over a shared occupancy belief, a division of the team into explorers and relays, a learned constraint mechanism that trades coverage against connectivity, and a training scheme that judges agents centrally but lets them act on local information alone. The experiments isolate, one at a time, whether the learned attention reads real structure, whether roles help, whether the connectivity constraint can be held without collapsing coverage, whether the value estimate transfers across team sizes, and—where a mechanism fails—whether the fault lies in the representation or in the learning signal. Throughout, we favour effect-based measurement: we ask what a mechanism changes about the mission, not whether it looks plausible.

Motivation

A single robot exploring a building is a solved problem in outline: drive toward the nearest unseen boundary, repeat. A team of robots is a different question, because the interesting behaviour is not in any one machine but in how their local choices compose into a group outcome. Send ten agents into a ruined structure to build a map, and two facts fight each other. Coverage wants them to spread—fan out, and the unseen area shrinks fastest. Communication wants them to stay close—radios reach only so far, and a map is worthless if the piece an agent holds never reaches the others. The team must therefore do something a lone robot never has to: manage its own connectivity as a resource, spending and recovering it while it works.

This tension is the whole subject. It is easy to satisfy either goal alone—a tight cluster is perfectly connected and covers almost nothing; a scattered swarm covers quickly and fragments into islands that can never share what they found. The research question is how a decentralized team, each agent acting on a keyhole view of the world, can hold both at once, and how the small decisions of individual agents propagate through the group to decide the mission. We call the individual-agent view the micro level and the mission-outcome view the macro level; the communication graph is the layer that couples them, and it is where most of our attention goes.

Two commitments shape everything below. First, the world has structure: walls, rooms, corridors, chokepoints. An open floor is a poor test, because on it connectivity is nearly free—agents can always shorten a link by moving in a straight line. Corridors and doorways make connectivity genuinely scarce, and they are where a relay agent earns its place. Our experiments run on generated room-and-corridor terrain for exactly this reason. Second, we insist on hard physical feasibility: two agents may never occupy the same cell. A collision-free guarantee is not decoration—it is what separates a policy that could drive real machines from one that only looks good in a plot.

Problem formulation

We model the task as a finite-horizon, cooperative, partially observed decision process shared by all agents—a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process, or Dec-POMDP [2]. Writing I for the set of agents, the process is the tuple

⟨ I, S, {Ai}, {Ωi}, T, O, R, ρ0, H ⟩,

where S is the true world state (agent positions, which cells are covered, where the walls are), each agent picks an action from its own set Ai (a move to one of the neighbouring cells, or stay), and receives an observation from Ωi rather than seeing S. Transitions follow T, observations follow O, the shared reward is R, the start state is drawn from ρ0, and the episode lasts H steps. Because the reward is shared, there is no competition between agents; the difficulty is entirely one of coordination under partial information.

The three layers

It helps to name three levels explicitly, because the mechanisms we test live at different ones.

Micro. At each step an agent i holds a local input xti—what its sensor sees within a small radius, plus auxiliary signals such as messages and its own role—and a memory hti of what it has accumulated. It chooses an action from a policy ati ∼ πi(· | hti). Nothing here refers to the group.

Bridge. The agents form a time-varying directed graph Gt = (I, Et), with an edge whenever two agents are within communication range. Along its edges they exchange messages, and each agent folds the messages from its current neighbours into its own belief. This graph is the only channel through which one agent's discovery can reach another; it is also the object whose health we must protect.

Macro. The mission is scored on the whole trajectory τ = (s0, o0, a0, …, sH) by the return J(π) = 𝔼[Σt R(st, at)]. The interaction graph touches the mission twice: it shapes what each agent knows when it decides, and it shapes how the individual moves compose into the next state of the world.

Connectivity as a measurable quantity

To manage connectivity we need a number for it. The natural one is the algebraic connectivity, or Fiedler value, λ2: the second-smallest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian L(Gt) [1]. It is zero exactly when the graph is disconnected and grows as the graph becomes more robustly linked, so it is a single scalar that captures "is the team one piece, and how firmly." We use it two ways, and keep them strictly separate. As a grading signal it is computed by the trainer from the true positions and never seen by the agents; the honest connectivity score of an episode is the fraction of its steps with λ2 > 0.5. As an agent-facing signal it is only ever estimated locally, from what an agent can see of its own neighbourhood, so that a policy cannot quietly depend on a global quantity it could not access on real hardware.

The objective, and how we grade it

Coverage is graded as the fraction of free cells the team has visited by the final step. Two subtleties matter. Because sensing has a radius of zero in our hardest setting—an agent covers only the cell it stands on—the theoretical maximum coverage in a fixed number of steps is well below 100%; a god's-eye planner caps out near 72%. We therefore read coverage as a fraction of that attainable optimum, not as a raw percentage, so that "good" means "close to what was possible," not "close to a number that was never reachable." Connectivity is graded by the Fiedler fraction above. A run that accrues coverage while quietly fragmenting is not a success, and the two-number report makes that visible.

The agent

The policy is a stack of parts, each with one job. We describe them from perception up to the decision, then say how they are trained.

Perception and the shared belief

An agent's raw sight is a small window, but its working memory is a full-map occupancy belief—a seven-channel picture of the world in the manner of a simple simultaneous-localization-and-mapping system. The channels record which cells are known to be free, which are known walls, which remain unknown, where the frontier between explored and unexplored lies, where the field boundary is, and where the agent and its neighbours sit. Crucially, this belief is gossiped: when two agents are in range they merge maps, so an agent that has personally seen nothing new still carries the discoveries of its neighbours. Frontier detection follows the classic idea of driving toward the boundary of the known region [3]. This is what makes the communication graph load-bearing rather than ornamental—cut it, and an agent is thrown back on its own keyhole.

The backbone

The belief is turned into features by a convolutional encoder, and the per-agent features are then refined by a graph network that passes messages between communicating neighbours. We adopt this perception–action–communication shape from work on learnable coverage control, whose defining property is that the same trained weights apply to a team of any size, because convolution and graph message-passing do not bake in a fixed number of agents [12]. Size-invariance is not a convenience here; it is the precondition for asking whether anything learned at four agents survives at ten.

The explorer head and its attention

Given features, an agent must choose where to go. The explorer head reduces the belief to a coarse sense of direction—for each of eight compass sectors, how much unexplored ground lies that way—and a small learned attention weights those eight directions to bias the chosen waypoint toward the most informative one. This is deliberately modest: the attention operates over eight hand-computed numbers, not over the map itself. Whether that modesty is a fatal limitation is one of the questions the program answers, and it is where a richer attention over the whole belief, in the manner of the transformer [16], would enter if the evidence demanded it.

Roles: explorers and relays

Not every agent should chase frontiers. Some should hold the graph together so that others can range further. We give the team a learned role selector that labels each agent an explorer (drive toward unexplored space) or a relay (position to keep a neighbour in range). This division of labour is the classic answer to the coverage–connectivity tension, and the idea that useful roles can be learned rather than hand-fixed—as readable latents, or as restrictions on which actions a role may take—comes from role-oriented multi-agent learning [13,14]. We test both whether the split helps and, when it falters at scale, why.

Holding connectivity: a learned constraint

How should the policy be pushed to keep λ2 up without simply ordering everyone to huddle? We treat it as a constrained-optimization problem and solve it with a Lagrangian penalty. A per-step term −λ·relu(θ − λ2) is subtracted from the reward whenever connectivity falls below a target θ; the policy descends on this shaped reward while the multiplier λ is itself adjusted upward when the constraint is violated and relaxed when it is satisfied. This is reward-constrained policy optimization [10], and we compare its plain dual ascent against a fixed penalty and against a proportional–integral–derivative controller on the multiplier, which can both raise and lower λ and so track the target more stably [11]. The point of sweeping the target and the mechanism is to see the multiplier move—to watch the price of connectivity rise until the constraint binds—rather than to assert that it works.

The critic, and why it must not count

Training uses centralized value estimation: a critic sees the whole team's state and scores it, while each policy still acts on local input alone—centralized training, decentralized execution. But a critic that hard-codes a fixed number of agents cannot transfer when the team grows. We therefore build the critic to be count-invariant: it encodes each agent, then pools the encodings with a permutation-invariant operator—a mean, or an attention—so its value does not depend on agent order or, in principle, on how many there are. The construction follows deep permutation-invariant sets [6] and their use as a multi-agent critic [7].

Credit: who did what

A shared reward tells the team it did well but not which agent helped. Spread across ten agents, that signal is thin. As an alternative we compute an exact difference reward for coverage—each agent is credited with the cells only it covered, Di = G − G−i, the team value minus the team value had agent i not acted—which is the marginal contribution and pays disjoint sweeping while starving redundant overlap [8,9]. Because coverage is submodular, this difference is exact and cheap to compute, not an approximation.

The controller and the collision guarantee

Heads produce a goal; a reactive controller turns it into an actual one-cell move. Two rules make the move physically admissible. A mask forbids stepping onto a cell a neighbour occupies right now. That alone is not enough—two agents can still claim the same empty cell in the same instant—so a deterministic priority rule resolves such ties: among agents converging on one cell, the lowest-indexed proceeds and the rest hold. Together these guarantee that no two agents ever share a cell, which we verify directly rather than trusting the reward penalty to discourage overlaps. The policies are optimized with proximal policy optimization [4] in its cooperative multi-agent form [5].

The experimental program

Five studies, each isolating one mechanism. The style throughout is to fix everything but the variable of interest, run several random seeds, and—where possible—write down in advance what each outcome would mean, so that the reading of the result is not invented after seeing it.

T1 — probing the mechanisms

Before asking whether the system performs, we ask how it works. Two probes freeze a trained policy at many moments and inspect one internal quantity. The first compares the explorer's attention against the truly most-open direction: does its favoured compass sector match the sector with the most unexplored ground, more often than chance? The second cuts the communication graph and measures how far the agent's belief and its chosen goal move: if messages are load-bearing, removing them should change decisions. These are diagnostic, not performance, measurements—they tell us about mechanism, and they are cheap enough to run on a single processor.

T2 — do roles help?

A clean two-arm comparison: a uniform team of identical explorers against a team split into explorers and relays, held fixed across a ladder of world sizes and repeated over several seeds. The prediction is that the split should buy connectivity—relays are exactly the agents whose job is to hold the graph—and the interest is in whether that advantage survives as the team and the world grow.

T3 — can the constraint be held?

This is the study of the connectivity mechanism itself. We fix the world at its hardest setting and vary two things: the mechanism (a fixed penalty; the learned dual; the PID dual) and the target θ that connectivity must clear. The measurements are the two curves that are the test—the multiplier λ(t) and the realized connectivity λ2(t) over training—read together with the coverage they cost. The arms:

  1. Fixed penalty. One constant weight for all scales — a brittle baseline that cannot adapt.
  2. Learned dual. The multiplier climbs by dual ascent when the target is violated; it auto-tunes but only ratchets upward.
  3. PID dual. A proportional–integral–derivative controller on the multiplier, which can raise and lower it, and so should track a moving target more stably.

The honest result is the coverage–connectivity frontier each traces, and whether the price of connectivity actually rises to meet the target. If the multiplier climbs but connectivity never reaches θ, that is itself the finding: local information is not enough, and a relay or a better connectivity estimate is needed.

T4 — does the value estimate transfer?

Three critics—a fixed-count convolutional one and two count-invariant pooling variants (mean, attention)—are each trained from scratch at three team sizes, and then the smallest-team critic is transplanted, without any further training, into the larger worlds. We report the error between its value prediction and the true discounted return, normalized so the numbers compare across scales. The hypothesis is sharp: the fixed critic's error should blow up as the team grows, while the count-invariant ones hold.

T5 — representation or signal?

T1 shows the eight-sector attention reading frontiers well at small scale and failing at large. Two explanations compete, and they demand different fixes. Either the eight-direction summary is too coarse to carry the needed distinctions at scale—a representation limit, which attention over the full belief would cure—or the learning signal is too weak: credit spread thin across many agents, and a hand-built frontier heuristic that already does the job, leaving the learned part nothing to earn. A two-by-two factorial separates them, crossing the explorer (the eight-sector head against a patch-wise attention over the belief) with the credit scheme (shared reward against the exact difference reward). Each outcome is assigned its meaning in advance:

If we observe…then the conclusion is…
the difference reward rescues the eight-sector headthe failure was in the signal; richer attention is unnecessary
belief-wide attention beats it at matched creditthe failure was in the representation; the richer head is warranted
belief-wide attention wins only with the difference rewardboth matter: capacity is inert without signal
nothing movesthe cause is elsewhere — re-diagnose rather than build

The strongest piece of evidence we already hold points at the signal: the eight-sector representation is the same at four agents and at ten, yet it works only at four. What changes between them is not the representation but the conditions under which it must be learned. T5 is designed to confirm or overturn that reading, and—only if the representation turns out to be the true bottleneck—to justify building belief-wide attention, which would double as the reading mechanism for any later deliberative layer.

Protocol and measurement

Every training run is defined by a single fixed specification, so that a difference in outcome can be attributed to the one axis under study and not to a drifting default. That specification pins the terrain to generated rooms-and-corridors, the horizon to one hundred steps, the sensing radius to its hardest setting, the seven-channel belief, the collision guarantee, and the connectivity mechanism; only the variable of interest moves. We run at least three seeds per configuration and report the mean with its spread; a result counts only if the band does not straddle the baseline it must beat.

Two measurement decisions carry weight. Coverage is graded as a fraction of the attainable optimum, because the raw percentage is capped far below one hundred by the horizon and the zero-radius sensing, and reporting the raw number would make every method look like a failure. Connectivity is graded by the fraction of steps whose true Fiedler value clears a threshold—computed by the grader, never by the agent—so a policy cannot inflate its score by acting on a global quantity it could not observe in the field.

ComputeThe studies run across two machines. A single large-memory accelerator carries the heaviest configurations one at a time, because at the largest team size a single training instance occupies most of the card; a second machine with two smaller accelerators runs the lighter configurations in parallel. This split is not incidental—the memory footprint of the largest setting is effectively irreducible at full training speed, so the fast machine is reserved for it while the smaller one absorbs everything that fits, and the two proceed independently.

Findings so far

Only the mechanism probes (T1) have returned; the performance studies are still running. Two results already stand, both on a single trained policy and so to be read as strong first indications rather than settled facts.

Finding 1 — communication is load-bearingCutting the message graph moves each agent's belief substantially and changes its chosen goal on roughly half of all steps, at both small and large team sizes. The team genuinely acts on what neighbours share; the graph is not ornamental, and it matters more, not less, as the team grows.
Finding 2 — the attention collapses at scaleThe explorer's attention points at the truly most-open direction about four times more often than chance at small scale, but only at chance at the largest—its "attention" there carries no information about where the unexplored space is. And even where it works, it overrides the underlying heuristic on only a small fraction of steps. The learned direction-finder is, at the scale we care about, close to inert. This is precisely the fact T5 exists to explain.

Related and inspiring work

The program stands on a body of work, and it is worth being clear about what we take from where. The architecture inherits its size-invariant perception–communication shape from learnable coverage control [12], and the surrounding literature on scale-invariant graph-attention exploration [18] and on learnable long-horizon exploration with an explicit goal-selection head [17] both motivates the direction and warns where the eight-sector head is likely to be the weak link. The role idea—that a division into explorers and relays can be learned rather than fixed—comes from role-oriented multi-agent learning, whether as readable latents [13] or as action-space restrictions [14]. The connectivity mechanism is constrained reinforcement learning [10] with a responsive dual controller [11], applied to the Fiedler value [1] in the spirit of connectivity-constrained multi-agent control [19]. The critic is a permutation-invariant set function [6,7]; the credit scheme is the difference reward [8,9]; the optimizer is cooperative proximal policy optimization [4,5] on a Dec-POMDP [2], with frontier-driven exploration [3] as the classical backbone of the coverage task.

One cross-cutting caution shapes the reading of T5. A well-known study of hierarchy in reinforcement learning found that its benefit comes chiefly from improved exploration rather than from the imposed structure itself [15]; the lesson we carry is to distrust the reflex to add machinery, and to demand that a richer representation prove itself against the cheaper hypothesis that the learning signal, not the architecture, was the limit. If belief-wide attention is eventually warranted, it will be as the transformer's attention over map tokens [16]—but only after the evidence has ruled out the simpler explanation.
